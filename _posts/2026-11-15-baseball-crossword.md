As a religious NYT Crossword solver, amateur constructor, and the go-to sports guy in my trivia-night groups, I’ve always wondered which athletes pop up in grids most frequently. Anecdotally, inclusion seems to be a function of three key characteristics: Are you famous? Does your first or last name make for solid fill – it’s 4-5 characters long and contains a mix of vowels & common letters (Shaquille O’Neal yes, Bobby Grich no)? And ideally, do you play in one of the media markets responsible for the bulk of crossword publication, especially New York? You can only stumble across ALOU so many times before you start asking questions.

I used Saul Pwanson’s [crossword corpus](https://xd.saul.pw/data) and searched for any words clued with a phrase matching “baseball” or “MLB” to create my initial list. However, this cast far too wide a net. Instead of MLB players, I just ended up with standard crossword-ese that just so happened to occasionally take a baseball clue. One 1952 NY Times cluing of TSAR as “Ford Frick’s role in baseball” did not mean all 3,185 uses of the word in the database qualified. And six minor-league ERIE references – five to the Double-A club based in Erie, PA plus one contrarian New Yorker reference to the Erie, OH indy-ball Crushers – didn’t register all 6,296 uses of the answer as baseball-themed.

Even slam-dunk baseball references in my mind received surprisingly diverse cluings. Just three of 62 ENOS clues in 2026 referenced Enos Slaughter or Enos Cabell in any way. Most referenced the Bible, some referenced For All Mankind actress Mirielle Enos, and one referenced a “1960s space chimp.” Wikipedia delightfully [points out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enos_%28chimpanzee%29) that Enos was “the only chimpanzee to orbit the Earth and the third hominid and primate to do so after cosmonauts Yuri Gagarin and Gherman Titov.”

While manually parsing these clues for baseball references would produce the most complete list, I just didn’t have time to go through thousands of clues per potential answer. So I took the easy way out: for a clue-answer pair to make it into my final dataset, the clue must contain either “baseball” or “MLB” in its text. Initially, I felt anxious that I’d systemically exclude non-MLB athletes with these criteria. However, I manually checked all clues containing “NPB” and “KBO” text strings and found just one baseball-related clue across the full set of matches, a Defector clue noting that many NPB stadiums have DOMES.

From there, I just had to exclude non-player names by hand. As you’d expect, team abbreviations that serve as helpful fill dominate the totals. Of eight answers with more than 100 occurrences, just three were player names; the other five were NAT, ASTRO, NATS, METS, and RBI in order, with ORIOLE and REDS just missing the cut. The three easiest ways to get your team abbreviation atop the list? Be one of baseball’s early franchises and buy yourself more time to accrue name-drops, exist in the Northeast where NY-and-DC-centric clue-writers will trust their readers to know your names, and have a name made up of useful crossword fill. The Nationals couldn’t have picked a better nickname if they tried.

Plenty of other non-player phrases necessitated filtering: GMS, which was used as shorthand for both “games” and “general managers,” SAC, ATBAT, and so on. But none of these were terribly interesting, so at risk of burying the lede, let’s move onto the players.

As you might guess, ALOU (197) took first place by a mile: three vowels and one common consonant make the surname extremely useful for fill, and it could reference not one but two very famous MLB players. First clued by the New York Times in 1972 as “family name in baseball,” Moises and Felipe were both commonly referenced by name. Ironically, the Alou family is actually the Rojas family: Alou is the matronym, not the surname, but the Giants scout who signed Felipe mistakenly recorded his last name. Extra credit to the AV Club for referencing this fact in a 2022 grid.

ENOS (94) and TRIS (66) are two other names you’d expect to make frequent crossword cameos: both contain useful letters, and Hall of Famers Enos Slaughter and Tris Speaker have been inductees for most of the archived history of crossword grids. Enos Slaughter racked up nine references in the NY Times puzzle between 1954 and 1976, before journeyman Enos Cabell displaced him over the course of his 15-year career: despite racking up just 11 bWAR, 77 percent of ENOS clues (10 of 13) between 1980 and 1986 used Cabell, not Slaughter, as the clue. However, after retirement, Enos Slaughter reclaimed his rightful throne: Cabell’s name hasn’t popped up since 2004. 

Mel OTT (62) follows a similar profile to Tris Speaker and Enos Slaughter: Hall of Fame pedigree and a short, common-letter-filled surname, with a career in New York to boot. His team affiliation inspires plenty of bad puns – ”Giant among baseball’s Giants,” for one – though I did learn from one clue that Ott debuted at just 17 years old, replacing Jimmie Foxx as baseball’s youngest player. FOXX, for his part, is hardly mentioned – while the surname pops up more frequently than you’d expect for a double-X word, with 85 references, Jamie Foxx and Redd Foxx dominate the cluing.

How could I not pair Bud SELIG (37) and AROD (36)? One of the steroid era’s biggest stars and the commissioner who made him the public face of PED malfeasance via the Biogenesis scandal land side by side, though if RODRIGUEZ entries are hand-counted, A-Rod takes a slim lead. His clues are rich with factoids: I learned that Rodriguez is the namesake of UMiami’s stadium, as well as baseball’s leader in grand slams. I also learned he was the first baseball player to be involved in an instant-replay decision, which is unfortunately [not true](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_replay_in_Major_League_Baseball). While A-Rod’s home run in September 2008 was the first official instant replay review, a 1999 Cliff Floyd double which hit the top of the scoreboard was reviewed by an umpire using a television monitor to confirm the call. The umpire, for the crime of being a decade ahead of the times, was immediately reprimanded by the league after a Marlins protest.

Up-the-middle legends CAL (32) Ripken Jr., STAN (30) Musial, Pee Wee REESE (21), ERNIE Banks (17) and RYNE (16) Sandberg all pop up a fair bit. For Ripken, most clues cite his iron-man streak. The nicknames “the Man” and “Pee Wee” are typical clues for Musial and Reese. Shockingly, only one Banks clue references his signature “let’s play two!” catchphrase – do better, folks.

For decades, ABNER (26) Doubleday was recognized as the inventor of baseball in the grid, with only occasional muted skepticism like ”reputed inventor of baseball” in the 1952 NYT. However, the tides of history have turned: the AV Club noted that Doubleday “almost certainly didn't invent” the sport in 2013, and Slate called his claim “bogus” in 2024. 

Willie MAYS and Hank AARON each receive 15 name-drops, though the last three baseball-themed clues for AARON have referenced Aaron Judge rather than Hammerin’ Hank. Legends of the game OREL (24) Hershiser, Pete ROSE (17), Joe TORRE (14) and David Ortiz – Big PAPI (13) – hover around this range. YOGI and BERRA are both clued 12 times, a nice symmetry. ICHIRO, HONUS Wagner, and Sammy SOSA each pop 11 times, though just three clues reference the famed T-206; since the average crossword player probably only knows Wagner from that anecdote, that paucity of clues surprised me. And former commissioner and Domino’s Pizza adviser Bowie KUHN pops 13 times. 

With the exception of Aaron Judge dislodging Hank Aaron as the go-to baseball AARON, though, there are precious few grid mentions of modern players. A manual crawl through 2026’s crosswords to date is depressingly empty. Three SALs crop up, but all referencing A’s slugger Sal Bando rather than scrappy World Series champion Sal Frelick. OHTANI pops up in just two puzzles – offensively, the same number of appearances as SAVANNAHBANANAS.

Other active players mentioned at least once in a 2026 crossword include Pete Alonso, Mookie Betts, Gerrit Cole, Elly de la Cruz and Kris Bryant. That’s only five, and one hardly qualifies as “active” at this point! Even more concerning, just one of these players debuted in the 2020s; exactly one single indexed “MLB” or “baseball” crossword clue this year matched a player who started their career this decade. One major caveat: I am almost certainly under-counting some real occurrences because of the strict filters I chose – for example, hand-searching Ohtani in all clues, not just those with “baseball” or “MLB” mentioned, reveals 4 matches, not 2. And this issue systemically affects current players, who solvers are more likely to associate with a particular team rather than the sport as a whole. Of six 2026 clues referencing Juan Soto, three mention the Mets, two reference the “2020 NL batting champion,” one clues him as an All-Star starter, and none explicitly mention “MLB” or “baseball.” 

A better analysis than mine would parse clue-answer pairs more thoroughly: one could create a more exhaustive set of potential regular-expression match searches, manually comb through all pairs of clues with potential MLB answers, or use a LLM to match answers to MLB-themed clues. The first option would remain non-exhaustive, the second would require a time-prohibitive amount of work, and the third is off the table because I can think of nothing more joyless.

So I’ll finish with three contemporary recommendations for crossword grid inclusion: 

* Reigning NL Cy Young Jacob Misiorowski, who put together an all-time great season and throws arguably the greatest fastball in baseball history. Nine 2026 JACOB clues referenced mayor Jacob Frey and the Twilight werewolf (valid) along with heartthrob Jacob Elordi (less valid). And wouldn’t a timely reference to a World Series Champion be far less overdone than “Les ___” for MIZ, as the NYT printed earlier this year?

* Surely we can replace the two CRIS clues of the year, both Collinsworth references, with Cristopher Sanchez references.

* National League MVP Pete Crow-Armstrong: the NL MVP fresh off a 10 WAR season. 2026 crosswords contain 39 PETE clues, heavy on references to professional irritant Pete Davidson and McKinsey veteran Pete Buttigieg. While 3-letter fill isn’t too common, PCA could also be a useful phrase for constructors.
